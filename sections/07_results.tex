\section{Results}

\subsection{Study A: Authoring Workflow}

Editable Semantic Performance Tags were associated with higher final-performance ratings in the complete task-level pairs. Mean match to the participant's intended reading increased from $5.07$ ($SD=0.90$) with Direct Generation to $5.97$ ($SD=0.83$) with editable tags ($W=10.5$, $p=.0077$, Holm-adjusted $p=.0117$, $r_{rb}=.80$, $n=15$). Final-performance satisfaction increased from $5.07$ ($SD=0.94$) to $6.03$ ($SD=0.85$; $W=4.0$, $p=.0058$, Holm-adjusted $p=.0117$, $r_{rb}=.90$, $n=15$). Figure~\ref{fig:study-a-task} summarizes these task-level outcomes.

Across the nine workflow items, editable tags were numerically higher on every item, but no item remained significant after Holm correction. The largest mean difference concerned revision clarity: Direct Generation $M=4.93$ ($SD=1.38$) versus editable tags $M=6.36$ ($SD=0.74$), $W=4.5$, raw $p=.018$, Holm-adjusted $p=.126$, $r_{rb}=.84$ ($n=14$). Creative ownership increased from $M=4.53$ to $M=5.73$ ($n=15$; Holm-adjusted $p=.053$, $r_{rb}=.92$), and dyadic coordination from $M=5.00$ to $M=6.07$ ($n=15$; Holm-adjusted $p=.087$, $r_{rb}=.89$). Raw NASA-TLX scores did not show a statistically reliable workload difference between workflows after Holm correction. Mean scores were slightly higher for editable tags on all six dimensions. Mental demand was $34.6$ ($SD=19.8$) for Direct Generation and $37.2$ ($SD=22.2$) for editable tags; effort was $29.1$ ($SD=22.1$) and $33.0$ ($SD=25.5$), respectively. Physical demand had the smallest unadjusted $p$ value ($8.8$ vs. $12.8$, raw $p=.047$), but this did not survive correction (Holm-adjusted $p=.285$). We therefore find no evidence that the editable representation reduced workload in this first-use study. Figure~\ref{fig:study-a-tlx} summarizes the six raw dimensions.

The three tag-specific diagnostics were positive and are reported descriptively. Participants rated the match between Semantic Performance Tags and their desired level of editing at $M=5.38$ ($SD=1.09$), the usefulness of phrase-level acting interpretation at $M=5.63$ ($SD=1.26$), and the dual-character layout for coordinating speaker and listener performance at $M=6.44$ ($SD=0.63$).

\subsection{Study B: Context Ablation}

Full-Context proposals received higher overall scene-fit ratings than Dialogue-Only proposals. Participant-level means increased from $4.80$ ($SD=1.18$) to $5.08$ ($SD=1.25$), with all non-zero participant differences favoring Full-Context ($W=0$, $p=.0049$, Holm-adjusted $p=.0246$, $r_{rb}=1.00$, $n=16$). This was the only criterion that remained significant after Holm correction. Intention ($4.86$ vs. $5.06$), affect ($4.68$ vs. $4.80$), attention/gaze ($4.85$ vs. $5.08$), and timing ($4.89$ vs. $5.13$; $n=15$) did not show corrected significant differences. Timing showed a comparatively large directional effect ($r_{rb}=.82$) but did not survive correction ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.064$). Thus, in the tested setup, added context changed the holistic appropriateness of a proposed performance more clearly than any single exposed semantic channel.

Across 96 scene-level preference questions, Full-Context was selected 45 times, Dialogue-Only 24 times, and ``No preference'' 18 times; seven responses were missing and two were ambiguous. Among the 69 determinate choices for one of the two proposals, Full-Context therefore accounted for 65.2\%. We treat this as descriptive support for the appropriateness result rather than as an independent inferential test. Figure~\ref{fig:study-b-preference} shows the observed preference counts.

\subsection{RQ3: Saved Semantic Edits and Logging Coverage}

The saved canonical-plan snapshots demonstrate that Semantic Performance Tags were not merely visual annotations: participant edits were written back to executable semantic values. For example, one saved session reduced an affect value from \texttt{Angered-70} to \texttt{Angered-50} while retaining the associated head action, and the edited plan marked the changed field as user-edited. Such records validate the representation-to-execution path, but logging coverage was not uniform enough across the early sessions to estimate reliable population-level acceptance, modification, or removal rates. We therefore do not convert incomplete plan histories into RQ3 frequencies.
