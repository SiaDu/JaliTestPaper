\section{Evaluation}

We designed a two-part evaluation to separate two questions that would otherwise be confounded: whether exposing AI-proposed semantic decisions as editable authoring objects changes how animators revise a performance (RQ2--RQ3), and whether additional conversational context changes the appropriateness of the proposed performance interpretation itself (RQ1). To match the progression from end-to-end use to semantic ablation in Figure~\ref{fig:study-design}, \textbf{Study~A} evaluates the authoring workflow in Maya, whereas \textbf{Study~B} evaluates text-only semantic proposals under Dialogue-Only and Full-Context input conditions.

\subsection{Participants and Overall Procedure}

We recruited 16 adults with relevant experience, education, or training in character animation, cinematic animation, technical animation, or a closely related creative field. Eligibility required familiarity with Maya or a comparable keyframe-animation package; professional experience was not required. Sessions were scheduled for approximately 85--100 minutes and participants received no financial payment, gift voucher, or course credit. The study was approved by Bournemouth University under Ethics ID~72575. Participants read the Participant Information Sheet and provided informed consent before beginning; interview audio was recorded only with separate consent.

Each session began with a brief orientation to the prototype, the two authoring workflows, and the task procedure, followed by one tutorial scene that was not included in the formal analysis. The tutorial focused on operating the interface and completing the task rather than teaching a preferred performance interpretation. The same participants completed both studies, and formal stimuli did not overlap between Study~A and Study~B. Participant-facing written materials and questionnaires were available in English or Chinese according to participant preference while preserving the same condition content and rating structure. Interviews were conducted in English or Chinese according to participant preference; quotations translated from Chinese are translated by the first author for reporting.

The prototype uses an external commercial LLM API during generation. Only prepared scene/dialogue material and non-identifying Acting Direction text required for generation were sent to this service. Participant-identifying information, questionnaire responses, interview responses, and audio recordings were not sent to the external AI service.

\subsection{Study A: Authoring Workflow Comparison}

\subsubsection{Tasks and Experimental Conditions}

Study~A evaluates whether exposing the AI-proposed semantic interpretation through editable Semantic Performance Tags changes how animators revise AI-assisted conversational performances. Participants completed one tutorial followed by four short formal dyadic scenes. Formal scenes were selected to exercise complementary context-dependent acting problems---including concealment or suspicion, restrained or affectively incongruent response, evolving interpersonal tension, and relationship repair---rather than a fixed set of emotion categories. Each formal scene contained exactly two speaking characters and was selected to remain comparable in overall duration and dialogue complexity. Participants authored the performance of \emph{both} interlocutors until the result matched their own intended reading of the scene rather than reproducing a predefined reference performance.

We used a within-subject comparison with two conditions. The implementation uses the same underlying generation and execution path in both conditions; the study UI mode changes whether the semantic-tag and interpretation sections are available for authoring.

\paragraph{Direct Generation.}
Participants provided the dialogue and Acting Direction and viewed the resulting JALI/Maya animation, but the intermediate semantic representation was hidden. If they wished to change the acting, they revised the natural-language Acting Direction and regenerated the complete proposal and animation. This condition operationalizes a prompt-and-regenerate workflow without exposing the semantic decisions that mediate generation.

\paragraph{Editable Semantic Performance Tags.}
Participants received the same initial dialogue, Acting Direction, model configuration, and generated animation, but could additionally inspect the two character-specific Semantic Performance Tag panels. They could change one or multiple executable decisions across either character---including affect, persistent or temporary attention, head behavior, and performative blink---before validating and applying the edited tags. These edits update the existing canonical plan and are then deterministically recompiled; they do not invoke a selective LLM regeneration step. The current system does not provide field locking or selective LLM regeneration.

The comparison holds execution variables constant. Both conditions use the same LLM configuration, scene audio, character rigs, cameras, JALI backend, and semantic look-at targets. To avoid confounding the authoring-representation comparison with shot setup, look-at targets are calibrated for each scene/camera before the formal task and the same captured calibrations are used in both conditions. Direct low-level Maya curve editing was excluded from the formal task so that differences primarily reflect prompt-level regeneration versus semantic-tag editing.

Each participant completed two formal scenes per condition. Scene-to-condition assignment and condition order were balanced across participants so that neither a particular scene set nor first-use order was systematically associated with one workflow.

\subsubsection{Interaction Records}

For each authoring task, the prototype retained the original generated canonical plan together with the final available authored plan, edited-plan snapshots where produced, and sidecar authoring-session records. These artifacts support plan-level reconstruction of semantic revision without requiring explicit ``accept'' or ``reject'' controls. We compare original and final semantic states by character and channel (affect, gaze/attention, head, and blink), including additions, value changes, placement changes where recoverable, and removals.

We operationalize an original AI proposal as \emph{preserved} when its semantic value and placement remain unchanged in the final authored plan, \emph{modified} when the proposal remains but its value or placement changes, and \emph{removed} when the original proposal is no longer present. User-added semantic decisions are analyzed separately. These categories are derived from plan differences rather than explicit user endorsement and therefore should not be interpreted as direct measures of agreement with the model.

The interface also records coarse inspection and semantic-edit events where available, such as opening or closing authoring sections and transitions between clean and edited tag states. Because the instrumentation does not provide a complete action-by-action history for every session, we use these records descriptively and do not infer complete revision sequences or precise task duration from them.

\subsubsection{Subjective Measures}

After each of the four formal scenes, participants answered two 7-point agreement items (\emph{1 = strongly disagree; 7 = strongly agree}): whether the final performance matched the way they intended to interpret the scene, and whether they were satisfied with the final performance they produced.

After completing the two tasks in each workflow condition, participants completed the six Raw NASA-TLX workload dimensions \cite{hart1988nasa}: Mental Demand, Physical Demand, Temporal Demand, Performance, Effort, and Frustration, each rated from 0 to 100. We compute the unweighted Raw TLX score as the mean of the six dimensions while retaining the individual dimensions for interpretation.

Participants additionally completed nine study-specific 7-point agreement items after each workflow block. These measured whether: (1) they could express the acting direction they wanted; (2) they could revise one part of the performance without unnecessarily changing parts they wanted to keep; (3) they understood which aspect of the performance they were changing when making a revision; (4) they felt in control of the final acting direction; (5) the workflow helped coordinate the speaking and listening performances of the two characters; (6) it was easy to move between evaluating the animation and making revisions; (7) the final performance felt like their creative decision rather than a decision made for them by the system; (8) the workflow allowed exploration of different ways the characters could perform the scene; and (9) the quality of the final result was worth the time and effort invested. The final two items were informed by the Exploration and Results Worth Effort dimensions of the Creativity Support Index \cite{cherry2014csi}. Because we did not administer the complete CSI, we report these items individually and do not calculate or interpret a validated CSI score.

Following the Editable Semantic Performance Tags condition, participants answered three additional diagnostic items concerning whether the semantic tags matched the level at which they wanted to make performance changes, whether the read-only interpretation-by-phrase view was useful when a proposed behavior was unclear, and whether the two-character layout made speaker--listener coordination easier. These condition-specific diagnostics are not used for between-condition statistical comparisons.

\subsubsection{Post-Study Interview}

We concluded the interactive authoring study with a semi-structured interview focused on the process behind observed revisions. Questions examined how participants decided that a performance was satisfactory, which AI-proposed decisions they preserved, changed, or removed, what they changed first when an animation did not match their scene interpretation, and how prompt-level regeneration compared with local Semantic Performance Tag editing for exploring alternatives and preserving satisfactory choices. We also probed dependencies among gaze, affect, head motion, and blink; coordination between speaker and listener performance; the usefulness of \emph{Acting Interpretation by Phrase}; the distinction between semantic gaze targets and shot- or rig-specific gaze realization; perceived authorship and control; semantic decisions that did not fit the current vocabulary; and desired future authoring operations. The complete interview guide is provided in the supplementary material.

\subsubsection{Study A Analysis}

For the two per-task ordinal outcomes---match to intended interpretation and satisfaction---we use cumulative-link mixed-effects models with workflow condition as a fixed effect and participant and scene as random intercepts. The nine condition-level 7-point items are compared within participants using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests; because these items are reported individually rather than as a composite scale, we control family-wise error across the nine comparisons using Holm adjustment and report paired effect sizes alongside medians and interquartile ranges.

Raw NASA-TLX is analyzed as a paired within-participant workload measure. We treat the unweighted Raw TLX score as the primary workload summary and report the six dimensions separately to identify the source of any workload difference; dimension-level comparisons are treated as secondary and Holm-adjusted. Behavioral records are used to characterize the granularity and location of semantic revision, including which character and semantic channels are most frequently preserved, modified, added, or removed. Coarse UI-session and inspection events are used descriptively rather than treated as a complete action sequence.

\subsection{Study B: Context Ablation}

\subsubsection{Stimuli and Conditions}

Study~B isolates semantic interpretation from animation execution. We constructed six short dyadic text vignettes using purposive maximum-variation sampling rather than selecting only cases in which additional context was expected to improve the proposal. The set varied in both contextual dependence and interactional function. Two vignettes used relatively explicit supportive or confrontational language and served as lower-context-dependence cases. Four higher-context-dependence vignettes involved concealment, face-saving, tentative affiliation, and relationship repair, where the same local dialogue could support substantially different performance interpretations once prior events or interpersonal relationships were known. Table~\ref{tab:study-b-stimuli} summarizes the sampling logic.

The vignettes contained no prescriptive facial-animation directions such as ``looks away,'' named expressions, head gestures, or blink instructions. Context descriptions specified only narrative facts, character relationships, and relevant prior events, leaving the performance interpretation to the model.

For each vignette we generated shared dyadic semantic proposals under two conditions while holding the production generation pipeline, model configuration, prompt schema, character identities, and decoding configuration constant. In the \textbf{Dialogue-Only} condition, the model received the labeled dialogue and character identities but no additional Acting Direction or narrative background. In the \textbf{Full-Context} condition, it received the same dialogue together with the vignette's narrative and interpersonal context through the Acting Direction field described in Section~4. We generated four independent Dialogue-Only/Full-Context pairs for each of the six vignettes, yielding 48 retained proposals in total. The four replicate pairs for each vignette were distributed across 16 preconstructed participant packets so that each replicate pair appeared equally often and Dialogue-Only versus Full-Context appeared first equally often across assignments.

Crucially, the ablation applied only to the \emph{model input}. Human raters always saw the complete vignette context and dialogue before judging the proposals. Each trial presented two anonymized text-only proposals labeled Plan~A and Plan~B. Each proposal showed the two character-specific Semantic Performance Tag projections together with their read-only acting interpretations by phrase, but no rendered animation. Condition labels and generation replicate identifiers were hidden, and first/second presentation order was counterbalanced.

\subsubsection{Study B Measures}

For each anonymous proposal, participants answered the following 7-point items (\emph{1 = not at all appropriate; 7 = extremely appropriate}):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Overall contextual appropriateness:} How appropriate is this proposed performance for the scene as a whole?
    \item \textbf{Acting interpretation:} How well does the proposal capture what the two characters are trying to do in this exchange?
    \item \textbf{Affect:} How appropriate are the proposed facial affect choices and intensities?
    \item \textbf{Attention:} How appropriate are the proposed attention/gaze choices, including engagement, avoidance, temporary checks, and targets?
    \item \textbf{Dyadic coordination and placement:} How appropriate is the placement of performance changes relative to what each character says or hears, including speaker--listener reactions?
\end{enumerate}

After rating both proposals, participants selected Plan~A, Plan~B, or No preference in response to: ``Which proposal would you prefer to use as the starting point for animating this scene?'' They then briefly described the most important difference, if any, that affected their choice.

\subsubsection{Study B Analysis}

Overall contextual appropriateness is the primary Study~B outcome; acting interpretation, affect, attention, and dyadic coordination/placement are secondary outcomes. We analyze the 7-point ratings using cumulative-link mixed-effects models with context condition (Dialogue-Only versus Full-Context) as the primary fixed effect and participant and vignette as random intercepts. Generation replicate pair is included as a random effect nested within vignette to account for variation among independently sampled model outputs. We additionally examine, as a pre-specified exploratory analysis, whether the context effect differs between the lower-context-dependence stimuli (S1--S2) and the higher-context-dependence stimuli (S3--S6).

For starting-point preference, anonymous Plan~A/Plan~B selections are remapped to their underlying generation condition. We report the frequency of Full-Context, Dialogue-Only, and No-preference choices and analyze non-tied preferences using a mixed-effects logistic model with participant and vignette as random intercepts. We report effect sizes and confidence intervals and do not interpret statistical significance alone as evidence that Full-Context proposals are universally preferable.

Interview responses and the brief open-ended Study~B explanations are analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis \cite{braun2006thematic,braun2019reflexive}. The first author iteratively codes the qualitative material, develops candidate themes, and revisits them against the full corpus, retaining divergent cases rather than requiring agreement across participants. Chinese responses are analyzed in the original language where applicable, with quotations translated into English by the first author for reporting. Qualitative themes are used to explain how participants interpreted and negotiated AI-proposed acting decisions rather than as a substitute for the quantitative comparisons.
