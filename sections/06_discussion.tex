\section{Discussion}

\subsection{When Does Context Change a Performance Interpretation?}

Study~B provides evidence that narrative and interpersonal context matters at the level of the performance as a whole. Full-Context proposals improved overall scene-fit ratings after correction, while the individual ratings for intention, affect, gaze, and temporal placement did not independently reach corrected significance. One interpretation is that context changes the coherence among several plausible choices rather than simply moving a single facial channel in a predictable direction. This is consistent with the design premise that contextual information constrains an acting space without establishing one uniquely correct performance. The starting-plan preferences point in the same direction, but the presence of no-preference and missing responses cautions against treating Full-Context as uniformly preferable.

\subsection{Performance Interpretation as an Authoring Object}

Study~A does not support a strong claim that exposing Semantic Performance Tags improves control, workload, or final quality. Final-performance ratings moved in a favorable direction and revision clarity showed the largest paired effect, but none of the workflow comparisons survived multiple-comparison correction in this ten-participant sample. The tag-specific diagnostics nevertheless suggest that participants generally understood the representation and especially valued the dual-character coordination view. These findings are better read as evidence that the semantic layer was usable as an authoring surface than as evidence of superiority over prompt-level regeneration.

The absence of a workload reduction is also informative. Editable semantic control introduces an additional object that must be inspected and revised. The present data therefore fit a control--effort trade-off more closely than an efficiency narrative. A larger counterbalanced study would be required to separate the value of semantic locality from practice and order effects.

\subsection{Semantic Attention Versus Shot-Specific Gaze Realization}

The system design separates the semantic attention relation from its shot-specific visual realization. Semantic tags express who or what a character attends to, while the Maya execution layer resolves that relation through calibrated look-at poses. This follows the broader tagged high-level control tradition used by JALI and S$^3$ \cite{edwards2020jali,pan2024s3}, but places an editable AI-proposed interpretation upstream of execution. Because Study~A held gaze calibration constant and did not evaluate calibration as an independent factor, we treat this boundary as a design implication rather than a user-study finding. It permits the same semantic attention decision to remain stable while its geometric realization changes with camera angle, staging, or rig.

\subsection{Interpretation Visibility Without Treating Reasons as Ground Truth}

Phrase-level acting interpretation received a mean diagnostic rating of 5.30 out of 7, indicating moderate-to-positive perceived usefulness, but lower than the 6.20 rating for the dual-character layout. This supports keeping the interpretation available as lightweight provenance rather than making explanation the center of the workflow. The representation communicates the system's proposed acting reading so that it can be accepted or contested; it does not establish that the proposal is correct or expose hidden model reasoning.

This framing is particularly important for character acting because several interpretations may remain defensible. Any broader implication for creative AI should therefore concern negotiation among plausible interpretations rather than model confidence or prediction correctness.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

The current results should be interpreted as an N=10 first-use snapshot. The collected Study~A sessions used a fixed Direct-Generation-then-editable-tags order. Practice, familiarity with the scenes and interface, or fatigue may therefore contribute to paired workflow differences; the study does not provide an order-independent causal estimate of the representation effect. The questionnaire analysis is also stronger for RQ1 and questionnaire-level RQ2 than for RQ3. Claims about which generated semantic decisions were retained, modified, or removed require separate original-to-final plan reconstruction from the saved canonical plans and session logs, so we do not infer acceptance or rejection rates from the questionnaire alone.

Several additional limitations are inherent to the present design. First, the study evaluates short dyadic scenes and a first-use workflow; it does not establish how the representation scales to long-form production, repeated professional use, overlapping speech, or scenes with more than two active characters. Second, execution is demonstrated through JALI and Maya, so the semantic representation may be more general than the specific control vocabulary and rig backend evaluated here. Third, the prototype exposes only a subset of acting decisions---visible affect, semantic attention, head behavior, and performative blink---and low-level Maya edits are not synchronized back into the Semantic Performance Tags or the canonical backend plan.

Fourth, the Study~A comparison holds camera-specific gaze calibration constant in order to isolate the authoring representation. The study therefore does not independently establish that manual gaze calibration improves gaze readability across camera viewpoints; that remains a system capability and a target for dedicated evaluation. Fifth, LLM outputs can vary across models, prompts, and software versions. Study~B should therefore be interpreted as evidence about the role of context within the tested generation setup, not as a claim that a particular model produces uniquely correct acting. Finally, acting interpretation is culturally, stylistically, and production-context dependent, and the current semantic vocabulary may not transfer unchanged across performance traditions.

Future work should examine multi-character coordination, alternative or branching semantic-performance proposals, explicit preservation constraints during regeneration, bidirectional synchronization between semantic decisions and low-level curve edits, longer-term professional use, and dedicated evaluation of camera-aware gaze calibration.
