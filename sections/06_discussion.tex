\section{Discussion}

\subsection{When Does Context Change a Performance Interpretation?}

Study~B provides evidence that narrative and interpersonal context matters at the level of the performance as a whole. Full-Context proposals improved overall scene-fit ratings after correction, while the individual ratings for intention, affect, gaze, and temporal placement did not independently reach corrected significance. Timing showed a relatively large directional effect but remained above the corrected threshold. One interpretation is that context changes the coherence among several plausible choices rather than simply moving a single facial channel in a predictable direction. This is consistent with the design premise that contextual information constrains an acting space without establishing one uniquely correct performance. Starting-plan preferences point in the same direction: Full-Context accounted for 65.2\% of determinate two-plan choices, while the substantial number of no-preference responses cautions against treating it as uniformly preferable.

\subsection{Performance Interpretation as an Authoring Object}

Study~A now shows a reliable task-level association between Editable Semantic Performance Tags and the quality of the final authored result as judged by the participant: both match to intended reading and final satisfaction remained significant after Holm correction. This is stronger than a purely descriptive usability result. However, the mechanism should be interpreted cautiously. None of the nine individual workflow items survived family-wise correction, even though all nine means favored the editable-tag condition and several effect sizes were large. In particular, revision clarity, creative ownership, and dyadic coordination showed strong directional differences, but the corrected tests do not establish that perceived control, locality, or coordination independently caused the higher final-performance ratings.

The fixed workflow order is important here. All participants used Direct Generation before editable tags, so practice with the scenes, interface, rigs, or performance vocabulary could contribute to the later condition. The result therefore supports the editable semantic layer as a promising authoring surface, but not an order-independent causal claim of superiority over prompt-level regeneration.

\subsection{Control Without a Workload Reduction}

The NASA-TLX results do not support an efficiency narrative. None of the six dimensions differed after correction, and mean workload scores were slightly higher for editable tags on all six dimensions. Semantic control adds an inspectable object that must itself be understood and manipulated. The present data therefore fit a control--effort trade-off more closely than a simple workload reduction: participants may gain a more direct intervention point without necessarily reducing first-use cognitive or interaction cost.

This distinction matters for creative AI tools. A useful intermediate representation need not minimize effort if it makes revisions more legible, preserves intentional choices, or produces a result that better matches the creator's reading. Future counterbalanced and longitudinal studies should separate initial learning cost from any production-time benefit after familiarity develops.

\subsection{Semantic Attention Versus Shot-Specific Gaze Realization}

The system design separates the semantic attention relation from its shot-specific visual realization. Semantic tags express who or what a character attends to, while the Maya execution layer resolves that relation through calibrated look-at poses. This follows the broader tagged high-level control tradition used by JALI and S$^3$ \cite{edwards2020jali,pan2024s3}, but places an editable AI-proposed interpretation upstream of execution. Because Study~A held gaze calibration constant and did not evaluate calibration as an independent factor, we treat this boundary as a design implication rather than a user-study finding. It permits the same semantic attention decision to remain stable while its geometric realization changes with camera angle, staging, or rig.

\subsection{Interpretation Visibility Without Treating Reasons as Ground Truth}

Phrase-level acting interpretation received a mean diagnostic rating of 5.63 out of 7, while the dual-character layout received 6.44. These ratings suggest that participants found both forms of representation useful, with particularly strong support for seeing the two interlocutors together. This supports keeping phrase-level interpretation available as lightweight provenance rather than making explanation the center of the workflow. The representation communicates the system's proposed acting reading so that it can be accepted or contested; it does not establish that the proposal is correct or expose hidden model reasoning.

This framing is particularly important for character acting because several interpretations may remain defensible. Any broader implication for creative AI should therefore concern negotiation among plausible interpretations rather than model confidence or prediction correctness.

\subsection{What the Saved Edits Do and Do Not Establish}

Saved edited canonical plans confirm that participants could change executable semantic values rather than merely annotate a suggestion. In the available records, user edits are preserved alongside the original value, allowing an individual modification to be reconstructed. This supports the system claim that the Semantic Performance Tag layer is an operational authoring object.

It does not yet support population-level claims about how often suggestions were accepted, modified, removed, or reinterpreted. The earliest sessions were not logged with uniform enough coverage to distinguish a genuinely retained suggestion from an unrecorded intermediate edit in every task. We therefore avoid turning partial plan histories into acceptance rates. A future deployment should store immutable proposal IDs and a complete event log for every semantic add, change, remove, regenerate, and restore operation.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

The study includes 16 participants but remains a first-use evaluation with a fixed Direct-Generation-then-editable-tags order. Practice, familiarity with the scenes and interface, or fatigue may therefore contribute to paired workflow differences; the study does not provide an order-independent causal estimate of the representation effect. One participant also had ambiguous double selections on one editable-tag task, reducing the task-level paired tests to $n=15$, while a small number of workflow and Study~B item responses were missing or ambiguous and were handled with item-wise complete cases.

The questionnaire analysis is stronger for RQ1 and questionnaire-level RQ2 than for RQ3. Claims about how often generated semantic decisions were retained, modified, or removed require complete original-to-final histories or event logs, which were not uniformly available across the full dataset. Several additional limitations are inherent to the design. First, the study evaluates short dyadic scenes and a first-use workflow; it does not establish how the representation scales to long-form production, repeated professional use, overlapping speech, or scenes with more than two active characters. Second, execution is demonstrated through JALI and Maya, so the semantic representation may be more general than the specific control vocabulary and rig backend evaluated here. Third, the prototype exposes only a subset of acting decisions---visible affect, semantic attention, head behavior, and performative blink---and low-level Maya edits are not synchronized back into the Semantic Performance Tags or the canonical backend plan.

Fourth, the Study~A comparison holds camera-specific gaze calibration constant in order to isolate the authoring representation. The study therefore does not independently establish that manual gaze calibration improves gaze readability across camera viewpoints; that remains a system capability and a target for dedicated evaluation. Fifth, LLM outputs can vary across models, prompts, and software versions. Study~B should therefore be interpreted as evidence about the role of context within the tested generation setup, not as a claim that a particular model produces uniquely correct acting. Finally, acting interpretation is culturally, stylistically, and production-context dependent, and the current semantic vocabulary may not transfer unchanged across performance traditions.

Future work should examine counterbalanced workflow order, longer-term professional use, complete semantic-edit event logging, multi-character coordination, alternative or branching semantic-performance proposals, explicit preservation constraints during regeneration, bidirectional synchronization between semantic decisions and low-level curve edits, and dedicated evaluation of camera-aware gaze calibration.
