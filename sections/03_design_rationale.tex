\section{Designing for Editable Performance Interpretation}

\subsection{Formative Interviews}

We conducted semi-structured interviews with five practitioners involved in authoring or directing character performance. Four had professional animation or CG-production experience spanning film animation, game cinematics, previs, and 3D game animation; one was a senior game designer. Participants were 26--33 years old with 2--10 years of relevant professional experience (Table~\ref{tab:formative-participants}). We included both animation and game-production perspectives because the proposed workflow concerns upstream performance direction as well as facial-animation execution.

Participants were recruited through email and professional or social-media networks. Interviews were remote, lasted approximately one hour, and were uncompensated; all participants provided informed consent under institutional ethics approval. We first asked about existing performance-authoring practices, including how participants interpret dialogue and make decisions about gaze, expression, head motion, and blinking, and where speech-driven automation becomes insufficient. Participants unfamiliar with comparable speech-driven or JALI-based animation saw a short orientation example. We then introduced design probes---structured semantic decisions, selective preservation/regeneration, and brief rationales---after the open-ended discussion so that existing workflow concerns could be distinguished from reactions to proposed features.

We analyzed the interviews using reflexive thematic analysis \cite{braun2006thematic,braun2019reflexive}. OpenAI ChatGPT assisted the initial organization of recurring concepts and candidate codes; the first author reviewed all codes against the complete transcripts, revised or merged them, verified reported interpretations, and iteratively organized them into themes. Divergent responses were retained rather than forced into consensus.

\subsection{Findings}

\paragraph{Performance begins with acting direction but is judged visually.}
Participants described performance decisions as beginning above animation curves: motivation, personality, emotional state, relationships, prior events, and subtext established a target before individual behaviors were authored. However, they differed on when textual semantics should appear. P2 and P4 preferred seeing the system's interpretation before animation, whereas P3 and P5 found visual output more immediate for judging whether an acting choice worked. P3 also noted that key poses can communicate performance more directly than textual descriptions, while P5 still found structured semantics useful for understanding and revision. Semantic representation therefore appeared most useful alongside visual feedback rather than as its replacement.

\paragraph{Performance depends on narrative and production context.}
Participants consistently linked expression and gaze to personality, internal state, relationships, prior dialogue, and dramatic purpose. Gaze was treated as intentional, with eye contact, aversion, and re-engagement related to attention, attitude, thought, or withdrawal. They also emphasized shot scale, camera direction, staging, rhythm, and rig controls: close-ups could motivate finer eye and blink design, while broader or dynamic shots reduced the value of subtle facial behavior. These observations identify a broader production context than the prototype attempts to model. The prototype therefore models narrative and interpersonal context while leaving shot- and rig-specific realization to Maya.

\paragraph{Useful automation requires preservation, but channels remain coupled.}
Participants generally treated generated animation as a starting point and valued retaining satisfactory choices while revising disputed ones. Yet selective editing did not imply perceptual independence: P3 emphasized that gaze, expression, head motion, and other facial behaviors may need coordinated changes. Participants were therefore more comfortable preserving high-level decisions or global properties such as strength than freezing every low-level channel independently.

\paragraph{Explanations should remain lightweight.}
Interest in explanations was mixed. Some participants valued brief reasons for unexpected acting choices because they helped diagnose the system's interpretation; others preferred correcting a visible problem directly. P4 valued reasoning when it clarified an unexpected choice, whereas P3 preferred correcting an incorrect result during production and P5 wanted only enough information to understand the system's intention. Verbose explanations were seen as potentially disruptive. This motivated concise acting interpretations that support inspection without becoming a mandatory reading step.

\subsection{Design Goals}

The findings motivated four design goals.

\textbf{DG1 --- Couple semantic decisions with visual feedback.}
The system should expose animator-meaningful performance concepts while allowing users to evaluate their realization in animation. The Semantic Performance Tag representation therefore functions alongside, rather than instead of, the Maya preview.

\textbf{DG2 --- Represent narrative and interpersonal state across both interlocutors.}
The system should preserve character identity, speaker/listener structure, preceding dialogue, relationships, and relevant scene information in one shared conversational interpretation. Because a conversational beat may be driven by what one character says and what the other hears, speaking and listening behavior are interpreted jointly rather than as unrelated target-character problems. Shot composition and rig-specific constraints remain outside the semantic model; for gaze, the semantic target is stable while the animator calibrates its shot-specific realization in Maya.

\textbf{DG3 --- Support local but coordinated revision.}
Allow a disputed acting or attention decision to be revised without replacing the complete performance. Revision occurs at the semantic level---for example by changing affect, persistent attention, or a temporary check---while deterministic execution handles coordinated low-level realization.

\textbf{DG4 --- Keep interpretation lightweight and available.}
Expose concise natural-language acting interpretations for the initial state and meaningful beats so animators can understand the intended performance without reading a verbose explanation for every low-level behavior. These descriptions communicate the proposed acting choice rather than hidden model reasoning.

\subsection{Interaction Scope and Semantic Performance Tags}

The resulting representation sits between narrative interpretation and low-level facial animation. We focus on dyadic conversation: both interlocutors belong to one shared semantic proposal rather than independently generated character outputs. Internally, the backend stores a canonical Performance Plan; Maya projects it as two character-specific \textit{Semantic Performance Tag} panels over immutable dialogue. The plan contains an initial state for both characters followed by sparse semantic beats anchored to dialogue.

Each initial state specifies open-language acting, visible affect and intensity, persistent attention, and optional head state. A semantic beat records a meaningful executable change for one actor at a dialogue-word anchor, combining an acting interpretation with at least one changed channel---affect, attention, head, or blink. Acting-only beats are omitted because they have no executable effect in the current prototype, and simultaneous changes for the same actor are grouped within one beat. Listener beats may be anchored to the earliest semantically sufficient cue in another character's utterance rather than waiting for a turn boundary.

Attention distinguishes persistent focus from a brief check. Persistent focus remains active until changed; a brief check temporarily redirects gaze before returning to the prior target. Targets may be either interlocutor, another person, an object/location, or a supported directional aversion. Directional choices are context-dependent acting priors rather than fixed psychological codes. Geometric look-at coordinates are deliberately excluded from the semantic representation and resolved only during deterministic execution, allowing one semantic attention relationship to be recalibrated across camera views, staging arrangements, or rigs.

Visible affect uses a closed executable JALI vocabulary with explicit intensity, while the acting interpretation remains open language; head and blink are optional supporting channels. Table~\ref{tab:semantic-performance-tags} summarizes the representation.

The representation is intentionally sparse: an absent channel means its semantic state does not change. It does not replace visual authoring or imply perceptual independence among facial behaviors. Instead, it exposes \textit{what} the animator wishes to change while leaving timing, rig coordinates, and curve generation to deterministic execution.
