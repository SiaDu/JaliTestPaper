\section{Introduction}

Character performance is shaped by more than the words a character speaks. In conversational animation, an animator interprets who is speaking, who is being addressed, what has happened earlier in the scene, the relationship between characters, and what a character may be attempting to communicate or conceal. These contextual interpretations influence performance choices such as when a character establishes or avoids eye contact, how strongly the head participates in a gaze shift, whether an expression is sustained or suppressed, and how a character reacts while listening \cite{kendon1967gaze,vertegaal2001gaze,andrist2013gaze}. A short utterance such as \emph{``I'm fine,''} may therefore support substantially different performances depending on whether it follows a casual question, an accusation, or an emotionally significant event. Authoring such performances involves not only producing motion, but translating narrative and interpersonal interpretation into temporally coordinated animation decisions.

Procedural and data-driven systems have substantially reduced the effort required to produce lower-level character motion. JALI generates expressive, editable speech animation from audio and transcripts, while VisemeNet produces animator-centric speech motion curves directly from audio \cite{edwards2016jali,zhou2018visemenet}. For conversational gaze, S$^3$ combines speech, a director-tagged transcript, and 3D scene context to generate controllable head--eye motion \cite{pan2024s3}; its transcript tags provide explicit high-level control, but require the desired behavior to be authored in advance. More recently, LLM-based systems have introduced natural-language animation generation and control: Huang et al. translate language into structured rig commands \cite{huang2023realtime}; Keyframer supports iterative 2D animation design \cite{tseng2025keyframer}; LogoMotion combines generation with localized motion controls \cite{liu2025logomotion}; and Narrative Motion Blocks combine natural language with direct manipulation through intermediate-level animation actions \cite{bourgault2025narrative}. These systems demonstrate that generative assistance and human refinement can coexist within animation authoring.

Related HCI work has increasingly moved beyond prompt-only generation by externalizing user intent or model decisions into manipulable representations. Misty exposes semantic diffs for selectively retaining generated UI changes \cite{lu2025misty}; Brickify reifies design intent as directly manipulable design tokens \cite{shi2025brickify}; AI-Instruments turns ambiguous prompts into reusable interaction objects \cite{riche2025ai}; and DreamGarden exposes an editable hierarchical plan between a prompt and generated game implementation \cite{earle2025dreamgarden}. Such systems reflect a broader shift in human-AI interaction toward coupling automated reasoning with direct manipulation, user correction, and incremental control \cite{horvitz1999mixed,amershi2019guidelines}.

Character acting introduces a distinct form of ambiguity within this design space. Recent work already shows that pretrained LLMs can infer context-dependent affect and generate facial behavior from narrative scenarios without task-specific training \cite{durupinar2025prompt}. The unresolved authoring problem is therefore not simply how to generate or edit motion. An undesirable performance may arise because the system inferred the wrong communicative intent, selected the wrong social target, treated avoidance as engagement, or coupled otherwise plausible facial behaviors under an interpretation the animator does not share. When that interpretive layer is hidden, the animator must infer why a behavior appeared before deciding whether to change the motion or the acting choice that motivated it.

We investigate whether this interpretive layer can itself become an authoring object. The system first constructs a shared dyadic semantic proposal: a single LLM call produces an initial performance state for both characters followed by sparse semantic beats anchored to the immutable dialogue. The backend stores this proposal as a canonical Performance Plan, while the participant-facing Maya interface projects it as two character-specific \textit{Semantic Performance Tag} panels over the same dialogue (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}). Each emitted beat belongs to one character and contains a concise acting interpretation together with at least one semantic channel that meaningfully changes, such as visible affect, attention, head involvement, or performative blinking. Attention distinguishes persistent focus from temporary checks and can refer to the interlocutor, another person, a scene object or location, or a directional aversion. Within the exposed vocabulary, animators may revise a single tag or several tags across either character before applying the edits and deterministically recompiling the current proposal into JALI and Maya controls. The LLM proposes an interpretation; the animator remains responsible for which semantic decisions are kept, changed, added, or removed.

We ask three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] How does conversational and narrative context affect the appropriateness of LLM-proposed character performance decisions?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] How does an editable Semantic Performance Tag representation affect animators' perceived control and their ability to iteratively refine AI-assisted character animation?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] How do animators accept, modify, reject, and reinterpret AI-generated performance suggestions during authoring?
\end{enumerate}

We evaluate the system in two complementary studies. \textbf{Study~A} is a within-subject authoring-workflow comparison between Direct Generation and Editable Semantic Performance Tags while holding the model, dialogue, Acting Direction, scene audio, character rigs, cameras, and animation backend constant. \textbf{Study~B} is a text-only context ablation comparing Dialogue-Only and Full-Context proposals across six dyadic vignettes. Together, the studies separate the effect of exposing semantic decisions for editing (RQ2--RQ3) from the effect of additional conversational context on the proposed interpretation itself (RQ1). We combine appropriateness ratings, workflow and workload measures, diagnostic ratings, and saved plan differences where the interaction record directly supports them. Because early-session logging coverage is incomplete, RQ3 is treated as exploratory rather than as a population-level frequency analysis of accept/modify/reject actions.

This work makes three contributions. First, we introduce a shared dyadic semantic representation that externalizes an LLM-proposed conversational performance as an initial state and sparse, dialogue-anchored acting beats spanning both speaking and listening behavior. Second, we present an author-facing Semantic Performance Tag workflow that lets animators revise one or multiple semantic decisions across both characters while keeping dialogue content immutable and execution deterministic in JALI and Maya. Third, through a 16-participant authoring study and context ablation, we show that editable tags are associated with higher final-performance ratings and that additional context improves holistic scene fit, while documenting the current instrumentation boundary for detailed accept/modify/reject analysis.
