\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Animator-Centric Facial Animation}

Facial animation research has long sought to reduce production effort while preserving animator control. JALI introduced an animator-centric procedural model for expressive lip synchronization, producing editable viseme motion curves that integrate with production facial rigs \cite{edwards2016jali}. VisemeNet extended this direction with a learned audio-driven pipeline that produces animator-centric speech motion curves while accounting for phonetic structure and motion style \cite{zhou2018visemenet}. JALI's later production workflow also used tagged text transcripts to inject directorial information such as language and facial emotion into large-scale animation generation \cite{edwards2020jali}. These systems exemplify a production-oriented philosophy in which automation provides structured motion while retaining explicit hooks for artistic control and subsequent refinement.

BEAT established an earlier language-to-behavior pipeline in which linguistic and contextual analysis drove synchronized nonverbal behavior \cite{cassell2001beat}. Together, these systems motivate a separation between higher-level behavioral specification and lower-level motion realization. Our work follows this separation, but focuses on how an AI-proposed interpretation can itself become an inspectable and editable authoring object before it is compiled into animation controls.

\subsection{Contextual and Controllable Gaze Animation}

Conversational performance depends on nonverbal coordination that cannot be reduced to speech articulation. Classic studies linked gaze to speaking, listening, attention, and turn management \cite{kendon1967gaze,vertegaal2001gaze}. In virtual characters, conversationally coordinated gaze produces stronger communicative responses than random gaze behavior \cite{garau2001impact}, while gaze aversion can signal turn management, cognitive effort, and interpersonal regulation \cite{andrist2013gaze}. Reviews of gaze animation further emphasize coordination among eyes, eyelids, head motion, attention, and affect \cite{ruhland2014look}.

Automated gaze generation has approached this coordination from several directions. Speech-driven systems have learned coupled head, eye, and eyelid motion from acoustic features, showing that speech rhythm and head dynamics provide useful cues for plausible gaze behavior \cite{le2012live}. Expressive gaze models have instead treated eye, head, and torso motion as communicative parameters whose timing and magnitude can convey affective state \cite{lance2010expressive}. These approaches establish that gaze is both temporally coordinated with speech and capable of carrying expressive meaning, but provide limited access to narrative or interpersonal interpretation.

Gaze aversion is also associated with attentional disengagement and internal attention. Abeles and Yuval-Greenberg found that gaze aversion can function as an active avoidance strategy during non-visual cognitive tasks \cite{abeles2017gaze}, while Servais et al. found that observers robustly associated upward-averted gaze with internally oriented attention \cite{servais2022gaze}. These results motivate directional aversion as a potentially useful expressive cue, but not as a deterministic code for a particular mental state. We therefore treat direction as an editable acting prior whose appropriateness remains context dependent.

S$^3$ is particularly close to our execution problem: it combines speech audio, a director-tagged transcript, and a 3D scene representation specifying character geometry and salient look-at targets to generate conversational head and eye animation \cite{pan2024s3}. Its modular pipeline predicts focus versus aversion from speech, refines gaze trajectories using scene salience, and realizes them through coordinated head--eye control. Importantly, S$^3$ supports transcript-embedded look-at, directional-aversion, and gaze-override tags, extending a tagged-transcript authoring tradition also used in JALI production workflows \cite{edwards2020jali,pan2024s3}. These tags let an animator specify a desired gaze behavior explicitly; S$^3$ also notes that emotional or cognitive information is not automatically inferred from the transcript and must presently be authored through such tags.

Our work builds on this separation between high-level directives and motion realization, but addresses an upstream authoring problem. Rather than requiring the animator to specify the desired gaze behavior in advance, the system uses narrative and conversational context to propose a semantic attention decision within a broader acting interpretation. Attention is represented as either a persistent hold or a temporary check toward an interlocutor, another person, an object or location, or a directional aversion. These choices remain inspectable and editable before deterministic execution. In this sense, transcript tags are an execution-level control representation, whereas the Semantic Performance Tag representation is the author-facing surface from which such controls are compiled through the canonical backend plan.

\subsection{LLM-Assisted Animation and Contextual Performance}

LLMs have recently been explored as high-level interfaces to animation. Huang et al. showed that natural-language instructions can be translated into structured commands for rigged characters \cite{huang2023realtime}. Keyframer combines LLM-generated animation code with iterative prompting, variants, and direct editing \cite{tseng2025keyframer}, while LogoMotion couples visually grounded generation with localized controls for motion, grouping, and timing \cite{liu2025logomotion}. Narrative Motion Blocks is particularly close to our interaction problem: it represents intermediate-level animation actions as timeline blocks that combine natural language and direct manipulation, bridging high-level ideas and low-level temporal properties \cite{bourgault2025narrative}. These systems demonstrate that intermediate abstractions and direct manipulation can make generative animation more controllable.

Closer to character acting, Prompt-to-Animation maps narrative scenarios through an OCC-based interpretation to temporally varying FACS Action Units, demonstrating that pretrained LLMs can generate recognizable context-dependent facial expressions without task-specific training \cite{durupinar2025prompt}. This work establishes that narrative-to-facial-animation reasoning itself is not our novelty. Our focus is instead on making the inferred acting interpretation editable before execution. Narrative Motion Blocks externalize \emph{actions}; our Semantic Performance Tag representation externalizes a shared conversational interpretation as an initial dyadic state and sparse dialogue-anchored \emph{performance beats}, separating what the characters are interpreted to be doing from how those decisions are ultimately realized as animation.

\subsection{Human-AI Creative Authoring and Intent Externalization}

The design of our authoring workflow connects to a broader history of creativity support and mixed-initiative interaction. Creativity-support tools emphasize rapid exploration, preservation of alternatives, and iterative refinement rather than one-shot automation \cite{shneiderman2007creativity}. Mixed-initiative interfaces similarly seek productive couplings between automated reasoning and direct user manipulation \cite{horvitz1999mixed}, and human-AI design guidelines stress making system actions understandable, supporting efficient correction, and enabling users to dismiss or refine AI behavior \cite{amershi2019guidelines}.

Recent generative-AI systems increasingly operationalize these principles through explicit intermediate representations. Misty exposes generated changes as a semantic diff that users can selectively retain or remove \cite{lu2025misty}. Brickify transforms ambiguous visual intent into reusable design tokens that can be directly manipulated \cite{shi2025brickify}. AI-Instruments reifies prompts as reusable interface objects and explicitly surfaces multiple interpretations of ambiguous intent \cite{riche2025ai}. DreamGarden externalizes a hierarchical plan that users can prune, extend, and revise \cite{earle2025dreamgarden}, while DynEx and Beyond Code Generation expose structured design alternatives and implicit decisions before committing to implementation \cite{ma2025dynex,zamfirescu2025beyond}. Piet similarly grounds a domain-specific authoring representation in professional creative practice \cite{shi2024piet}. Together, these systems show that the value of an intermediate representation lies not in its existence alone, but in whether it exposes decisions at a level that matches how practitioners conceptualize their work.

This issue is also tied to agency in human-AI creative workflows. Studies of co-creative systems report that initiative and control shift across iterative creative processes rather than remaining fixed \cite{wan2024secondmind,zhou2024nonlinear}. Different placements of AI assistance can change cognitive activity and perceived agency even when the underlying task remains the same \cite{xu2025productive}. Our work therefore treats the Semantic Performance Tag representation not merely as a display of structured model output, but as a negotiation surface: the AI proposes a situated interpretation, while the animator can inspect and revise one or multiple semantic decisions before execution. In contrast to generic plans, design tokens, or code diffs, the representation is specific to conversational acting and explicitly distinguishes \emph{what the characters are interpreted to be doing} from \emph{how those decisions are realized as animation}.
