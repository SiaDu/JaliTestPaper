\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Animator-Centric Facial and Speech Animation}

Facial animation systems have long sought to reduce the labor of producing synchronized and expressive character performance while preserving the ability of animators to refine the result. JALI introduced an animator-centric procedural model for expressive lip synchronization that maps speech to editable viseme motion curves and is designed to integrate with production facial rigs and subsequent manual refinement \cite{edwards2016jali}. VisemeNet extended this direction using a three-stage recurrent architecture to generate animator-centric speech motion curves directly from audio while accounting for phonetic structure, speech style, and animator-specific motion profiles \cite{zhou2018visemenet}. Together, these systems demonstrate a production-oriented approach in which automation provides structured facial motion rather than replacing animator control.

Such systems primarily derive performance from properties of the speech signal and predefined procedural or learned mappings. However, deciding how facial and gaze behaviors should contribute to a particular dramatic performance remains partly an interpretive task. The same utterance may require different gaze, affect, or head behavior depending on the preceding interaction, the addressee, or the character's communicative intention. Our work builds on animator-centric procedural animation but focuses on the upstream problem of translating conversational and narrative interpretation into semantic performance decisions before those decisions are instantiated as animation.


\subsection{Conversational Gaze and Virtual Character Behavior}

Gaze is not merely a source of visual liveliness but an important component of conversational coordination. Early studies of dyadic interaction showed systematic relationships between gaze direction, speaking and listening behavior, and turn-taking \cite{kendon1967gaze}. Later work demonstrated that gaze direction can provide strong cues about conversational attention and addressee selection. Vertegaal et al.\ found that, in multiparty conversations, the person being looked at was frequently the person being listened to or addressed, suggesting that gaze can function as an indicator of conversational attention \cite{vertegaal2001gaze}. These findings motivate representing gaze targets in terms of conversational participants rather than only geometric eye directions.

For animated conversational characters, gaze behavior that is coordinated with interaction has also been shown to affect perceived communication quality. Garau et al.\ compared random gaze with gaze behavior informed by conversational turn-taking and found that conversationally informed gaze produced substantially better responses than random gaze behavior \cite{garau2001impact}. Beyond mutual gaze, gaze aversion itself carries communicative meaning. Andrist et al.\ modeled gaze aversion from recordings of human dyadic conversations and showed that its timing can support functions including turn management, signaling cognitive effort, and regulating interpersonal engagement \cite{andrist2013gaze}. Reviews of gaze animation similarly emphasize the interaction between eye, eyelid, head, attention, emotion, and conversational context in the synthesis of believable virtual agents \cite{ruhland2014look}.

Conversational behavior generation has also been approached at a higher semantic level. BEAT maps typed text to synchronized nonverbal behaviors using linguistic and contextual analysis together with rules derived from human conversational behavior \cite{cassell2001beat}. This work established an important precedent for generating multimodal character behavior from language rather than treating gesture, gaze, and facial motion independently. Our work shares the goal of grounding nonverbal behavior in conversational context, but focuses on a different authoring problem: making the system's inferred performance interpretation visible and editable. Rather than directly selecting a behavior from linguistic rules or committing generated gaze to animation, our Performance Plan represents social targets, avoidance and re-engagement, communicative intent, and timing as semantic decisions that an animator can inspect and revise before execution.


\subsection{LLM-Assisted Animation and Character Performance}

Large language models have recently been explored as interfaces between natural-language intent and animation systems. Huang et al.\ demonstrated that LLMs can translate natural-language instructions into structured commands for controlling rigged characters, establishing the feasibility of using language models as high-level interfaces to animation parameters \cite{huang2023realtime}. Keyframer applies LLM code generation to 2D animation design, combining natural-language prompting with direct editing and design variants to support iterative exploration \cite{tseng2025keyframer}. Similarly, LogoMotion uses visually grounded code synthesis to generate semantically meaningful logo animations and exposes code-connected controls for editing motion, grouping, and timing \cite{liu2025logomotion}. These systems show that useful LLM-assisted animation workflows need not end at generation: direct manipulation and iterative refinement can remain central to the creative process.

Closer to character performance, Prompt-to-Animation investigates zero-shot generation of facial animation from narrative scenarios \cite{durupinar2025prompt}. The system asks LLMs to interpret situational descriptions using the OCC emotion framework and translate these interpretations into temporally varying FACS Action Units. Its results demonstrate that pretrained LLMs can infer context-dependent affect and produce recognizable facial expressions without task-specific training. This is particularly relevant to our work because it establishes that narrative-to-facial-animation reasoning itself does not require a newly trained model.

Our focus differs in the role assigned to the generated interpretation. Rather than treating inferred affect or motion parameters as the final output, we treat the interpretation underlying a performance as part of the authoring process. In conversational acting, an animator may disagree not only with the magnitude or timing of a generated movement, but with assumptions about communicative intent, gaze target, avoidance, engagement, or the relationship between multiple behaviors. We therefore investigate how such semantic performance decisions can be externalized before execution, allowing the animator to revise the interpretation rather than only the resulting motion.


\subsection{Human-AI Creative Authoring and Intermediate Representations}

Research on human-AI creative tools increasingly emphasizes interaction structures that allow users to intervene between high-level intention and generated output. DreamGarden, for example, decomposes a high-level description into a hierarchical action plan and exposes this plan to users through interactions such as pruning, extending, and providing feedback \cite{earle2025dreamgarden}. Rather than functioning solely as an invisible reasoning process, the intermediate plan becomes a point of interaction between human intention and semi-autonomous generation.

Domain-specific creative interfaces similarly demonstrate that effective authoring support depends on exposing representations aligned with how practitioners conceptualize their work. Piet derives its interaction design from formative interviews with professional motion-graphics designers and provides representations and controls tailored to progressive color-authoring workflows \cite{shi2024piet}. Keyframer and LogoMotion likewise indicate that combining generative assistance with direct manipulation can support exploration and iteration beyond prompt-only interaction \cite{tseng2025keyframer,liu2025logomotion}.

These works motivate our treatment of intermediate representations as authoring objects, but character performance introduces a different type of intermediate state. A game-development plan represents actions to be implemented, while animation parameters describe how motion should be executed. In contrast, character acting also involves a subjective interpretation of why a character behaves in a particular way within an interaction. We operationalize this layer through a \textit{Performance Plan} that represents communicative intent, affect, gaze target and timing, avoidance and re-engagement, head involvement, and supporting narrative evidence. The contribution is therefore not intermediate representation or editability in isolation, but the use of a domain-specific semantic representation to make an LLM's interpretation of conversational performance inspectable and negotiable before it is translated into low-level animation.
