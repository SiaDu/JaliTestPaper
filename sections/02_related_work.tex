\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Animator-Centric Facial Animation}

Facial-animation research has long sought to reduce production effort while preserving animator control. JALI introduced an animator-centric procedural model for expressive lip synchronization with editable viseme curves, while VisemeNet learned audio-driven speech motion in an animator-friendly representation \cite{edwards2016jali,zhou2018visemenet}. JALI's later production workflow also used tagged transcripts to inject directorial information such as language and facial emotion at scale \cite{edwards2020jali}. BEAT established an earlier language-to-behavior pipeline in which linguistic and contextual analysis drove synchronized nonverbal behavior \cite{cassell2001beat}. Together, these systems motivate a separation between higher-level behavioral specification and lower-level motion realization. We retain that separation but focus on an upstream question: how an AI-proposed acting interpretation can itself become an inspectable and editable authoring object before it is compiled into motion controls.

\subsection{Contextual and Controllable Gaze Animation}

Conversational gaze is tied to speaking, listening, attention, and turn management \cite{kendon1967gaze,vertegaal2001gaze}. In virtual characters, conversationally coordinated gaze produces stronger communicative responses than random gaze, while gaze aversion can signal turn management, cognitive effort, and interpersonal regulation \cite{garau2001impact,andrist2013gaze}. Reviews further emphasize that plausible gaze depends on coordination among eyes, eyelids, head motion, attention, and affect \cite{ruhland2014look}. Automated systems have approached this coordination from complementary directions. Speech-driven methods learn coupled head, eye, and eyelid motion from acoustic features, showing that speech rhythm and head dynamics provide useful cues for plausible gaze \cite{le2012live}. Expressive models instead parameterize eye, head, and torso motion so that timing and magnitude can carry affective meaning \cite{lance2010expressive}. These approaches establish gaze as both temporally coordinated with speech and semantically expressive, but provide limited access to narrative or interpersonal interpretation.

Gaze aversion is also associated with attentional disengagement and internal attention. Abeles and Yuval-Greenberg observed active gaze avoidance during non-visual cognitive tasks, while Servais et al. found that observers associated upward-averted gaze with internally oriented attention \cite{abeles2017gaze,servais2022gaze}. We therefore treat directional aversion as a context-dependent editable prior, not a deterministic psychological code.

S$^3$ is particularly close to our execution problem: it combines speech, a director-tagged transcript, and 3D scene context to generate conversational head--eye animation, supporting transcript-embedded look-at, directional-aversion, and gaze-override tags \cite{pan2024s3}. Its modular pipeline predicts focus versus aversion from speech, refines trajectories using scene salience, and realizes them through coordinated head--eye control. Such tags explicitly specify desired behavior; S$^3$ also notes that emotional or cognitive information is not automatically inferred from the transcript. Our system addresses the upstream authoring decision. Narrative and conversational context are used to propose semantic attention within a broader acting interpretation, distinguishing persistent focus from temporary checks toward an interlocutor, another person, an object/location, or directional aversion. These choices remain editable before deterministic execution. Thus transcript tags function as execution-level directives, whereas Semantic Performance Tags expose the proposed interpretation from which those directives are compiled.

\subsection{LLM-Assisted Animation and Contextual Performance}

Recent systems use LLMs as high-level animation interfaces. Huang et al. translate natural-language instructions into structured rig commands \cite{huang2023realtime}; Keyframer supports iterative 2D animation generation, variants, and direct editing \cite{tseng2025keyframer}; LogoMotion combines generation with localized controls for motion, grouping, and timing \cite{liu2025logomotion}; and Narrative Motion Blocks externalize intermediate animation actions as manipulable timeline blocks \cite{bourgault2025narrative}. These systems demonstrate that generation and human refinement can coexist through intermediate abstractions.

Closer to character acting, Prompt-to-Animation maps narrative scenarios through an OCC-based interpretation to temporally varying FACS Action Units, showing that pretrained LLMs can generate recognizable context-dependent facial expressions without task-specific training \cite{durupinar2025prompt}. Narrative-to-facial reasoning is therefore not our novelty. Our focus is making the inferred acting interpretation editable before execution. Whereas Narrative Motion Blocks externalize actions, our Semantic Performance Tags externalize a shared conversational interpretation as an initial dyadic state and sparse dialogue-anchored performance beats, separating what the characters are interpreted to be doing from how those decisions become animation.

\subsection{Human-AI Creative Authoring and Intent Externalization}

Creativity-support and mixed-initiative work emphasizes rapid exploration, iterative refinement, understandable system actions, and efficient user correction \cite{shneiderman2007creativity,horvitz1999mixed,amershi2019guidelines}. Recent generative systems operationalize these principles through editable intermediate representations. Misty exposes semantic diffs \cite{lu2025misty}; Brickify reifies design intent as manipulable tokens \cite{shi2025brickify}; AI-Instruments turns prompts into reusable interaction objects \cite{riche2025ai}; DreamGarden exposes editable hierarchical plans \cite{earle2025dreamgarden}; and DynEx, Beyond Code Generation, and Piet expose structured alternatives or domain-specific authoring representations before final implementation \cite{ma2025dynex,zamfirescu2025beyond,shi2024piet}. Across these systems, the intermediate representation is not valuable merely because it exists; it matters when it exposes decisions at a level that matches how practitioners conceptualize and revise their work.

This issue is also tied to agency. Studies of co-creative systems show that initiative and control shift across iterative workflows, while the placement of AI assistance can alter cognitive activity and perceived agency \cite{wan2024secondmind,zhou2024nonlinear,xu2025productive}. We therefore treat Semantic Performance Tags as a negotiation surface rather than merely structured model output: the model proposes a situated conversational interpretation, and the animator can revise one or multiple semantic decisions before execution. Unlike generic plans, design tokens, or code diffs, the representation is specific to conversational acting and distinguishes \emph{what the characters are interpreted to be doing} from \emph{how those decisions are realized as animation}.
